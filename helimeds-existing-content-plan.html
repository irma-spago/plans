Existing content recovery plan

The 107 existing Helimeds blog posts got 2,733 clicks from Nov 10 to Nov 25, down 73% from 10,090 in the 16 days before. This plan removes 18 posts, consolidates 22 into stronger pages and refreshes the other 67, a few each weekday over nine weeks.

What happened

  1. A two-day drop. Sitewide clicks went 650 (Nov 11) → 331 (Nov 12) → 212 (Nov 13) and have stayed at 175–245 a day since. Existing content went from about 630 clicks a day to about 116. The timing falls inside Google's November 2026 core update (started Nov 10, ran about 14 days). Search Console shows no manual action.
  2. Broad health queries were hit hardest. Existing posts target competitive terms such as “bluechew side effects”, “sermorelin dosage” and “tadalafil side effects”. Many dropped off page 1, for example tadalafil-side-effects 8.1 → 19.5, nmn-vs-nad 7.8 → 28.4 and guide-to-ozempic-injection-sites 5.4 → 40.4. Narrow questions such as storage and interactions held their positions.
  3. Peptide content fell 77%. 27 peptide posts, mostly dosing guides for substances that aren't FDA-approved. Helimeds doesn't offer a peptide treatment, so nothing on the site backs them up.
  4. Thin sourcing and outdated facts. Several posts link to only 1–2 outside sites (for example how-to-take-blue-chew and can-you-take-tadalafil-and-sildenafil-together). 22 posts were first published in 2024, including the compounded GLP-1 posts written during the 2024 drug shortages, and the article schema has no dateModified, so Google can't see when anything was updated.
  5. Two posts Google treats as empty. Search Console marks how-long-ozempic-stay-in-your-sistem and ozempic-with-high-blood-pressure-medication as soft 404s from crawls in early September, so they aren't indexed. Both now have full articles and need a recrawl after their refresh.
  6. Misspelled drug names in URLs. Nine posts, for example aildenafil-vs-adalafil, does-sidelafil-expire and how-long-ozempic-stay-in-your-sistem. Seven older renamed URLs also return 404 with no redirect.
  7. Same-day timestamps. All 107 posts were uploaded on just two dates (Oct 15 and Oct 26). Spreading the refreshes over nine weeks gives each post its own real update date.

08 / Standards

What every refresh includes

A refresh only counts if a reader can see what changed

  1. At least 5 primary sources. FDA labels, PubMed trials, NIH and CDC, cited where each claim is made.
  2. Correct drug names in URL, title, heading and body.
  3. Current facts. Prices, savings programs, compounding status and approved uses as of the edit date.
  4. Reviewer whose specialty fits the topic, with a visible “Medically reviewed by … on [date]” line that changes when the post is reviewed again.
  5. Answer first. The direct answer in the first 2–3 sentences, then detail. Filler sections and repeated FAQs cut.
  6. Safety and limits. What isn't known, who shouldn't take it, when to see a clinician. No dosing protocols for unapproved substances.
  7. Internal links to the matching Helimeds treatment page and the closest related posts.
  8. Real “Updated” date on the page, in the sitemap and as dateModified in the article schema (it's missing today), changed only for this edit.
  9. Request indexing in Search Console after each refresh, so Google recrawls it within days instead of weeks.

How we'll measure it

  • Each week. Clicks, impressions and position for that week's posts, 14 days after the refresh vs 14 days before.
  • Early signal. Refreshed posts regain impressions and move back toward their October positions within 3–4 weeks.
  • Main signal. Existing content climbs back toward 300+ clicks a day, most likely around Google's next core update.
